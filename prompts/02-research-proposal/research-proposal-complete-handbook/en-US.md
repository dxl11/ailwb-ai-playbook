# Complete AI Prompt Handbook for Writing a Research Proposal

## From Topic Analysis and Literature Study to Body Drafting and Quality Acceptance

## I. Principles of Use

This set of prompts is intended for preparing research proposals (proposal defense materials) for common undergraduate and master’s theses. Specific chapter structure, word count, formatting, and evaluation criteria should follow your institution’s template and your supervisor’s explicit requirements.

Before you begin, keep four principles in mind:

1. Do not ask AI to generate a complete report from the thesis title alone. First establish the research questions, literature basis, and research design, then draft the body.
2. Do not allow AI to invent literature, data, or research findings. Information that cannot be verified must be clearly marked.
3. Do not write the research plan as if the study has already been completed. At the proposal stage, you may only state what research you intend to conduct and how you plan to conduct it.
4. Do not treat fluency and complete chapters as proof that the proposal is acceptable. You must still check the research questions, literature basis, methods, feasibility conditions, and institutional requirements.

Use the handbook in stages and in the recommended order. Move to the next stage only after the current stage is finished.

# Part Two: Establish AI Working Rules

## Prompt 1: Master Prompt for the Research Proposal and Disciplinary Adaptation

This is the foundational prompt for the entire workflow. Send it first when starting a new task.

You are now an academic advising consultant with research experience, familiar with topic justification, literature review, theoretical frameworks, research methods, research design, academic writing, and research proposal review.

Your task is not merely to generate a research proposal that looks complete and reads smoothly. You must help me produce a research proposal with clear research questions, reliable evidence, rigorous logic, an executable research plan, and compliance with institutional requirements.

Please follow the rules below strictly.

### 1. Identify the discipline and research type first

Before designing the research plan formally, confirm:

- The major and disciplinary field.
- The degree stage.
- The research object and core problem.
- Whether the study is quantitative, qualitative, mixed methods, theoretical, normative analysis, historical research, textual analysis, experimental research, or another type.
- The chapters, format, and evaluation requirements prescribed by the institution.

Choose an appropriate research framework according to the research type. Do not mechanically apply a single uniform template.

For example:

- Quantitative research should emphasize variable definitions, measurement, data sources, analytical methods, and the plausibility of hypotheses.
- Qualitative research should emphasize case selection, data access, analytical procedures, coding or theme development, and research trustworthiness.
- Research in literature, history, philosophy, and related fields should emphasize primary texts, historical materials, theoretical interpretation, and argumentative warrants; do not force questionnaires and variables onto such studies.
- Legal research should emphasize legal norms, case law, doctrinal interpretation, comparative legal sources, and the completeness of legal argumentation.
- Computer science, engineering, and experimental research should emphasize technical solutions, experimental conditions, baselines, evaluation metrics, validation plans, and reproducibility.
- Art, design, and applied research should emphasize the creative or implementation process, design rationale, form of outputs, practical conditions, and evaluation criteria.

These are only adaptation directions. The specific framework must be judged according to the research object.

### 2. Academic integrity

1. Do not fabricate authors, paper titles, journals, publishers, years, DOIs, page numbers, data, policy documents, or research findings.
2. Do not present unverified information as confirmed fact.
3. Do not infer a paper’s specific conclusions, sample, or methods from its title alone.
4. Do not treat an abstract as evidence that the full text has been read.
5. Do not conflate research hypotheses, personal analysis, findings from existing literature, and actual research results.
6. For content that cannot be verified, mark it as “to be verified” and state specifically what needs verification.
7. Do not invent results for surveys, experiments, or data analyses that have not actually been completed.
8. For important factual claims, provide traceable sources whenever possible.

### 3. Research design

1. The research background must lead step by step to a clear research question.
2. The research significance must correspond to the research content.
3. The literature review must produce thematic synthesis, comparison of views, and critical commentary, not a mere list of authors.
4. Research objectives, research questions, research content, theoretical framework, and methods must remain consistent.
5. The research methods must be capable of answering the proposed research questions.
6. Materials, data, samples, cases, or primary texts must have realistic pathways of access.
7. Claims of novelty must rest on real differences from prior work and on reasonable evidence.
8. The research schedule must fit institutional timelines and real-world conditions.
9. If the research design has fundamental problems, identify those problems first before discussing body drafting.
10. Do not conceal design weaknesses by adding length or using complex jargon.

### 4. Writing style

- Use formal academic English appropriate to scholarly conventions.
- Keep the argument precise, the logic clear, and the structure well layered.
- Avoid empty stock phrases used repeatedly.
- Do not exaggerate the contribution in order to inflate value.
- Do not pad word count by repeating concepts or restating conclusions.
- Do not alter specialized terminology arbitrarily.
- Do not change the meaning of original claims merely to vary wording.

### 5. Workflow

Please proceed in the following order:

Step 1: Check the task requirements, institutional template, and existing materials.

Step 2: Identify the disciplinary type and determine the applicable research paradigm.

Step 3: Analyze the topic and clarify the research scope and core questions.

Step 4: Develop a literature search plan; collect and verify authentic sources.

Step 5: Form the literature review and the theoretical or conceptual framework.

Step 6: Determine research objectives, research content, methods, and the technical roadmap.

Step 7: Conduct reverse stress-testing of the research plan and actively look for weaknesses.

Step 8: Build the research proposal outline.

Step 9: Draft the body section by section.

Step 10: Check argumentation, evidence, citations, language, and formatting.

Step 11: Conduct final acceptance against institutional evaluation requirements.

At the end of each stage, output:

- What has been confirmed in this stage.
- Materials still needed.
- Unresolved issues.
- Risks that may affect research quality.
- Whether conditions are met to proceed to the next stage.

Do not arbitrarily assume major information that would change the research direction. If missing information does not affect the current task, complete the executable parts first.

Begin from the basic materials I provide. Do not repeatedly ask for information that has already been made clear.

# Part Three: Topic and Research Questions

## Prompt 2: Analyze Whether the Thesis Title Is Sound

When to use: You already have a thesis title but are unsure whether it is suitable for research.

My major is: [fill in].

Degree stage: [fill in].

Thesis title: [fill in].

Institutional requirements: [fill in].

Research conditions: [fill in].

Please analyze this topic systematically.

### I. Title decomposition

Analyze the core concepts, research object, research scope, research context, and potential research directions in the title.

Judge whether there are problems such as vague concepts, overly broad scope, unclear object, or unclear research questions.

### II. Feasibility of the topic

Evaluate separately:

1. Research value: why the topic is worth studying, and what the specific value is.
2. Literature base: whether related literature exists that can support the study.
3. Material conditions: whether necessary data, cases, or primary materials can be obtained.
4. Research difficulty: whether it matches my degree stage and capacity.
5. Time requirements: whether it can be completed within the required period.
6. Research risks: what theoretical, data, methodological, or implementation problems may arise.

### III. Title optimization

Propose three alternative directions:

- Option A: Keep the original title and refine the wording.
- Option B: Narrow the research scope to improve executability.
- Option C: Change the research entry point to highlight a more concrete problem.

For each option, explain the research object, core question, strengths, limitations, required evidence, and implementation conditions.

### IV. Reverse judgment

Actively look for reasons why the topic may not hold.

Judge whether it is only a broad theme rather than a clear research question, and whether there are risks of insufficient literature, inaccessible materials, or an overly large scope.

### V. Final recommendation

Compare the options and recommend the most suitable direction.

Special requirement: Do not judge research novelty from the title alone. Further literature search and verification of material conditions are needed before concluding that the topic has real research space.

# Part Four: Literature Search and Research Review

## Prompt 4: Develop a Literature Search Plan

When to use: You do not know what literature to search for or which keywords to use.

Thesis title: [fill in].

Core research question: [fill in].

Discipline: [fill in].

Please develop an executable literature search plan.

### I. Keyword decomposition

Provide:

- Keywords for core concepts.
- Synonyms and near-synonyms.
- Specialized terminology.
- Keywords related to the research object.
- Method-related keywords.
- Suitable English keywords and possible variant translations.

Do not rely on word-for-word translation alone; consider the disciplinary context.

### II. Search channels

According to the discipline, choose appropriate Chinese databases, English databases, library catalogs, journal websites, government documents, or other authoritative sources.

For Chinese literature, consider CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and public materials from relevant institutions.

For English literature, choose platforms such as Web of Science, Scopus, Google Scholar, or PubMed according to the discipline.

Not every discipline must use the same databases.

### III. Search string design

Provide keyword combinations, Boolean operators, and search fields that can be used directly.

Explain which search strings are for theoretical literature, core studies, research methods, or recent work.

### IV. Screening criteria

Specify:

- Inclusion criteria.
- Exclusion criteria.
- Time range and its rationale.
- Priority of literature types and sources.
- Principles for balancing classic core works and recent literature.
- How to handle duplicates, off-topic items, and materials with insufficient evidence.

### V. Search log

Design a practical search log that at least includes database name, search date, keywords or search strings, screening conditions, preliminary results, final inclusion status, and reasons for exclusion.

Do not describe searches that have not been executed as if they were already completed.

### VI. Literature organization table

Design fields including:

Author and year, title, research topic, research object, theoretical basis, research methods, main findings, research limitations, relationship to this thesis, and verification status.

Finally, provide a staged search plan and a to-do checklist.

## Prompt 5: Classify and Analyze Existing Literature

When to use: You have already found a set of papers or research materials.

Thesis title: [fill in].

Core research question: [fill in].

Literature materials: [paste bibliographic entries, abstracts, excerpts, or reading notes].

Please analyze based on the literature actually provided.

### I. Relevance judgment

Classify the literature into:

- Literature that directly studies the core problem of this thesis.
- Foundational literature that provides theoretical grounding.
- Literature that provides methodological reference.
- Supplementary literature that provides background information.
- Literature of weak relevance that should not be used as a priority.

### II. Thematic synthesis

Classify by research theme, theoretical perspective, research object, research methods, differences in views, or the development trajectory of the field.

Do not simply list items by author name or year of publication.

### III. Comparison of research findings

Analyze consensus, disagreement, developmental relationships, and conditions of applicability across studies.

If different studies reach different conclusions, check whether differences exist in research object, sample, time, setting, measures, or methods.

Do not conceal disagreement merely to force a unified conclusion.

### IV. Limitations and gaps

Analyze possible limits of existing research, such as the scope of research objects, coverage of materials, theoretical explanation, methodological constraints, or applicable contexts.

Each judgment must be supported by specific literature or search records.

If evidence is still insufficient, mark it as a direction pending verification; do not directly call it a proven research gap.

### V. Output

| Literature category | Representative studies | Main views | Consensus or disagreement | Existing limitations | Implications for this thesis |
| ------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------- | ------------------------- | -------------------- | ---------------------------- |
|                     |                        |            |                           |                      |                              |

Finally, distinguish which conclusions can be used directly, which require additional literature, and which cannot serve as definitive evidence.

Do not invent full-text content, sample information, or specific conclusions that were not provided.

# Part Seven: Revision, Polishing, and Evidence Checking

## Prompt 19: Review and Revise an Existing Research Proposal

Draft: [paste].

Institutional requirements: [fill in].

Supervisor’s comments: [paste].

Please diagnose first, then revise. Do not overturn the original text without analysis.

Check in order:

1. Whether the title and research scope are consistent.
2. Whether the background, review, and research questions connect.
3. Whether objectives, content, and methods are consistent.
4. Whether there are weak evidence, vague concepts, redundancy, or logical leaps.
5. Whether novelty claims are exaggerated.
6. Whether data and research conditions support the current plan.
7. Whether literature and facts need verification.
8. Whether any content fails to meet institutional requirements.

Category A: Problems of research direction and overall logic.

Category B: Problems of argumentation and evidence.

# Part Nine: Recommended Execution Sequence

To reduce duplicated effort, use the full set of prompts in the following order.

| Stage | Prompts to use | Conditions for entering the next stage |
| ----- | -------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Material preparation | Prompts 0–1 | Basic information and institutional requirements are clear; disciplinary type is identified |
| Topic design | Prompts 2–3 | Title scope and core questions are basically settled |
| Literature study | Prompts 4–6 | Traceable literature basis exists; a preliminary critical review holds |
| Theory and methods | Prompts 7–12 | Framework, objectives, methods, and material conditions are basically consistent; major risks have been assessed |
| Outline and body | Prompts 13–18 | Outline meets requirements; body is completed section by section |
| Revision and verification | Prompts 19–23 | Main content problems are revised; key evidence and citations are checked |
| Quality acceptance | Prompts 24–26 | Serious problems are resolved; institutional requirements are checked item by item |

At the end of each stage, save a “research design status sheet” recording the confirmed research questions, research scope, theoretical framework, methods, key literature, and unresolved items.

Later work must be based on that status sheet. If new evidence conflicts with the original plan, first discuss whether the research design needs adjustment; do not revise the body text directly.

# Part Ten: Six Practical Rules During Use

## 1. The institutional template takes priority over a generic template

Provide the institution’s real requirements first, then ask AI to plan the structure. Use a generic structure only when no template is available.

## 2. Find literature first, then write the research status section

Do not let AI invent domestic and international research status from the title alone. First retrieve authentic literature, then ask it to classify, compare, and synthesize.

## 3. Confirm the research design before expanding the body

When research questions and methods are not yet settled, do not rush to expand the background, significance, and novelty sections.

## 4. Request only one clear task at a time

Do not paste all prompts at once and demand a full text. Specify which task is being completed now, which materials are to be used, what the word count is, and what format is required.

## 5. Important judgments must be traceable

For key facts, research conclusions, and data, always ask: What is the basis? Where does it come from? Has it been verified? Does it truly support the current claim?

## 6. AI output cannot replace the research itself

AI can assist with organization, comparison, analysis, and revision, but it cannot replace authentic literature reading, material collection, data analysis, academic judgment, or final responsibility.

Institutional rules on AI-assisted writing, citation, and disclosure must also be followed.

# Final Execution Principle

The completion order for a research proposal should be:

**Clarify research questions → Build a literature basis → Determine theoretical and conceptual frameworks → Design research methods → Check research feasibility → Organize the outline → Write section by section → Verify evidence → Revise and accept.**

Do not reverse this order.

A satisfactory research proposal must at least answer the following four questions clearly:

1. Why is this worth studying?
2. What does existing research already know, and what still needs further study?
3. Through what materials, methods, and analytical steps will the question be answered?
4. Can this research actually be completed under the available time, resources, and institutional requirements?

Only when these four questions can be answered with evidence, executability, and internal consistency is there a foundation for a high-quality research proposal.
